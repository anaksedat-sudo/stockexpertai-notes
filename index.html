Sector-Relative Stock Scoring: Eight Questions

Answers to the questions people ask most often when they first see a 0-100 stock score that is ranked inside the company's own sector.

1. What does a score of 80 actually mean?

It means the company sits, on average across five pillars, around the 80th percentile of its own sector. It does not mean an 80 percent chance of anything. It is a rank, not a probability.

2. Why compare inside the sector instead of the whole market?

Because market-wide ranking mostly ranks industries. Sort every stock by growth and the top decile fills with software; sort by yield and it fills with REITs. Ranking inside the sector removes that base rate so the number describes the company.

3. Can an 80 in banks be compared with an 80 in semiconductors?

Yes, and that is the point. Both mean top quintile of their own neighborhood. Comparing raw ratios across those two sectors would not be meaningful; comparing sector-relative percentiles is.

4. What are the five pillars?

Business quality, financial safety, valuation, growth durability and price momentum. Each is built from two or three metrics, each metric is converted to a within-sector percentile, and the pillars are combined into the total.

5. How often does a score change?

The momentum pillar moves with prices, so daily. The other four move when new filings arrive, so roughly quarterly. Every score carries a calculation date for this reason.

6. Why is the calculation date important?

A score built on the previous quarterly report looks identical to one built on the latest report. Without the date, a stale score cannot be told apart from a fresh one.

7. Where does the method break?

Small peer groups make percentiles noisy. Deep cyclicals at the bottom of their cycle score badly right before earnings recover. Data providers post fundamentals with a lag. Some sector labels mix two different business models. Each of these is a reason to look at the components, not only the total.

8. Does the AI produce the numbers?

No. In this design the figures come verbatim from market data providers and the language model only writes the commentary around them. The model never generates a number.
